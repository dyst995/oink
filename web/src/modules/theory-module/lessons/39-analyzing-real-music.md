# Analyzing real music

**Prerequisites:** Diatonic harmony; functional progressions; secondary dominants; borrowed chords; arpeggios and chord tones  
**Next:** Transposition and creation

## Concept

Analyzing means turning what you hear or see into a description that explains it: the key, the chords as Roman numerals, what each chord is doing, and how the melody relates to the harmony. The aim is to find patterns you can reuse, not to attach labels for their own sake.

Analysis is often ambiguous. Two analyses can both be defensible. The skill is to give a reading, justify it with evidence (notes, bass, ending, ear), and say what would change your mind.

## Underlying logic

### A workable procedure

1. **Find the tonic.** Listen for the note or chord that feels like home. Check the last chord, the first chord, and where the bass settles. Check the key signature if you have notation.
2. **Write the chords** with full names, including quality and any slash bass: `Am7`, `D7`, `C/E`.
3. **List the notes of the key** and check each chord for notes outside it.
4. **Write Roman numerals** relative to the tonic.
5. **Explain every chord.** Each chord is one of: diatonic, a secondary dominant, borrowed from the parallel mode, a substitute (such as a tritone sub), a passing or chromatic chord, or part of a modulation.
6. **Mark function and cadences:** where the harmony departs, builds tension, and returns.
7. **Analyze the melody:** label each melody note on strong beats as a chord tone, a passing note, a neighbor, or a held tension.
8. **Test with your ear:** play the chords, sing the roots, and check that your reading matches what you hear.

### Evidence for the key

The chord set test: write the chords you have and ask which major key contains all of them diatonically. Example: `G C D Em` all belong to `G` major (and to `E` minor). `C F G Am Dm` belong to `C` major. If one chord does not fit, check whether it is a secondary dominant or a borrowed chord before you change the key.

### Chord names and function

- **The lowest note is not always the root.** `C/E` is `C` major over an `E` bass. `Am7` and `C6` can share notes, so decide from context and bass.
- **A `7` chord is not always `V7`.** `A7` in a blues in `A` is `I7`. An `E7` in `C` major could be `V7/vi`.
- **A chord's function depends on what it does and where it goes**, not just how it is spelled.

### Ambiguous cases

`Em C G D` can be `vi IV I V` in `G` major or `i ♭VI ♭III ♭VII` in `E` minor. Evidence: if `Em` sounds like home and the loop feels at rest on it, the `E` minor reading is stronger. If `G` sounds like home and `D` pulls to it, the `G` major reading is stronger. The melody and the bass usually decide.

### Melody against chords

For each strong-beat melody note, name its relation to the chord. Over `G7`, `F` is the seventh, a chord tone. Over `Cmaj7`, `F` is the fourth, a non-chord tone that usually wants to move to `E`. Over `Fmaj7`, the same note `F` is the root.

## Musical significance

Analysis turns songs you like into knowledge you can reuse. If you see `I vi ii V` or `ii V I` in one piece, you can recognize it in another. It also helps you decide what to play: knowing a bar is `V7/ii` tells you which notes to target when you improvise.

## Guitar application

### Worked examples (original patterns, not transcriptions)

| Chords | Key | Numerals | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Gmaj7 / E7 / Am7 / D7` | `G` | `Imaj7 / V7/ii / ii7 / V7` | `E7` is a secondary dominant leading to `Am7` |
| `C / Am / F / Fm / C / G7 / C` | `C` | `I / vi / IV / iv / I / V7 / I` | `Fm` is borrowed from `C` minor; `A♭` falls to `G` |
| `Am / Dm / E7 / Am` | `A` minor | `i / iv / V7 / i` | `E7` uses `G♯` (harmonic minor) |
| `E7 A7 E7 E7 / A7 A7 E7 E7 / B7 A7 E7 B7` | `E` | `I7 IV7 V7` blues | `E7` and `A7` are dominant-quality chords that do not act as dominants of `E` |

### Finding chords on the neck by ear

1. Hum or find the tonic on a string, then find the bass note of each chord (usually on strings 6 to 4).
2. Test major or minor by finding the third: if the note a major third above the bass fits, the chord is major; a minor third means minor.
3. Check for a seventh by listening to whether the chord wants to move.
4. Play it with a shape you know and compare with the recording.

### Melody check

Play a four-chord loop and sing or play only the chord tones (roots and thirds) as a melody. Then add passing notes and say which melody notes land on chord tones.

## Exercises

1. Analyze `D | Bm | G | A` with numerals. Answer: `D` major, `I vi IV V`.
2. Analyze `Dm | Gm | A7 | Dm` and say which note is outside the `D` natural minor scale. Answer: `i iv V7 i` in `D` minor; the `C♯` in `A7` is outside natural minor (it comes from harmonic minor).
3. Analyze `C | E7 | Am | F | G | C`. Answer: `I V7/vi vi IV V I`; `E7` tonicizes `Am`.
4. Use the chord set test: which major key contains `Bm`, `E`, `F♯m`, and `A`? Answer: `A` major (as `ii`, `V`, `vi`, `I`).
5. Take any song you can play. Write its chords, key, and numerals, then mark one chord that is not diatonic and explain it as a secondary dominant, a borrowed chord, or something else.
6. Over `Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7`, a melody plays `A F E` on beats 1 of each bar. Label each note's relation to its chord. Answer: `A` is the fifth of `Dm7`; `F` is the seventh of `G7`; `E` is the third of `Cmaj7`.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** list the steps of an analysis and say why the lowest note and the chord symbol alone do not give function.
- **Written:** produce Roman numerals for an eight-bar progression and explain each chord as diatonic, secondary dominant, borrowed, or other.
- **Written:** label strong-beat melody notes against chords as chord tones or non-chord tones.
- **Fretboard:** find the chords of a simple unfamiliar progression by ear on the neck, using roots and thirds.
- **Aural:** identify the tonic by ear and hear whether a progression resolves or stays open.
- **Application:** analyze a piece you chose and use the analysis to plan an improvisation over it.
