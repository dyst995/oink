# Functional progressions

**Prerequisites:** Diatonic harmony  
**Next:** Tuning and the open strings

## Concept

In a key, diatonic chords group by **function** — the job they do relative to the tonic:

| Function | Typical chords (major key) | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tonic | I, vi (sometimes iii) | Home / rest |
| Predominant (subdominant) | IV, ii | Depart from home; set up dominant |
| Dominant | V, viiø | Tension that wants tonic |

**V → I** is the core pull. V7 contains the leading tone (degree 7) and, with the ♭7 of the chord, a tritone that resolves into tonic chord tones. That is why a dominant chord sounds like it “needs” to go home.

Common progressions are paths through these functions:

- **I – IV – V – I** — basic tonal loop; blues and countless songs use I–IV–V.
- **ii – V – I** — standard approach to tonic; predominant then dominant then home.
- **vi – IV – I – V** — pop “sensitive” loop; starts on tonic-relative minor.
- **12-bar blues** — I for four bars, IV for two, I for two, V–IV–I (with variations); often all dominant-quality chords even on I and IV in blues practice.

Analysis question for any short progression: which chords feel like home, which prepare, which pull? Roman numerals answer that once the key is known.

## Examples

- In `C`: `C – F – G – C` is I–IV–V–I.
- In `C`: `Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7` is ii–V–I.
- In `G`: `Em – C – G – D` is vi–IV–I–V.
- Blues in `A`: bars built on `A7`, `D7`, `E7` — still I, IV, V functions even when every chord is a dominant 7.

## Exercises

1. Write I–IV–V–I and ii–V–I in `E♭` major with chord symbols.
2. Explain in two sentences why `G7` wants `C` (or `Cmaj7`) more than `Gmaj7` wants `C`.
3. Pick a song you know. Find a stretch of four chords, name a likely key, and label Roman numerals (best guess is fine — check against the melody’s tonic).

**Done when:** you can assign tonic / predominant / dominant roles in a major key, build I–IV–V and ii–V–I in any key, and state why V7 resolves to I.
