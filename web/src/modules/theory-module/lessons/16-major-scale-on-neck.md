# The major scale across the neck

**Prerequisites:** Intervals on the neck; major scale; degrees  
**Next:** Triad construction

## Concept

A major scale on guitar is **seven pitch classes repeated everywhere they occur**, not a shape. The method is:

1. Spell the scale (major-scale construction).
2. Find every occurrence of each of its pitch classes on each string.
3. Choose a region and play the notes found there.

Shapes and "boxes" are the result of step 3, not the starting point. If you learn shapes first, you can play but cannot say what you are playing. If you learn construction first, the shapes appear on their own and you can rebuild them in any key.

## Underlying logic

Standard tuning (string 6 to 1) is `E A D G B E`. Adjacent open strings are a perfect fourth (5 frets) apart, except strings 3–2 (`G→B`), which are a major third (4 frets). Any pattern that crosses the 3–2 pair shifts by one fret. That one irregularity is the reason shapes look different on different string pairs.

On any single string, the scale's notes sit at fret offsets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12` from a root fret. Each note appears in a different place on every string, because each string starts at a different pitch.

Within a four-fret window, one string holds two or three scale notes: the scale has no gap larger than a whole step, so a four-fret span always catches at least two notes. Where the window holds three notes versus two depends on where the half steps (3–4 and 7–8) fall.

## Musical significance

Playing a scale while naming degrees links the formula to the sound. Knowing the scale in several regions frees you from one position: melodies and chord tones can follow the music up and down the neck instead of being trapped in a fixed shape.

A shape is valuable once you know what it is made of. It is one region of the full map. Knowing the map lets you change region whenever the music asks.

## Guitar application

`C` major (`C D E F G A B`) written string by string in frets 0–12. Each note comes from the open string's pitch plus half-step counting.

| String | Scale notes as note:fret |
| --- | --- |
| 6 (`E`) | `E:0 F:1 G:3 A:5 B:7 C:8 D:10 E:12` |
| 5 (`A`) | `A:0 B:2 C:3 D:5 E:7 F:8 G:10 A:12` |
| 4 (`D`) | `D:0 E:2 F:3 G:5 A:7 B:9 C:10 D:12` |
| 3 (`G`) | `G:0 A:2 B:4 C:5 D:7 E:9 F:10 G:12` |
| 2 (`B`) | `B:0 C:1 D:3 E:5 F:6 G:8 A:10 B:12` |
| 1 (`E`) | `E:0 F:1 G:3 A:5 B:7 C:8 D:10 E:12` |

Strings 6 and 1 match because both are `E`.

To see a "shape" emerge, choose a window. Frets 7–10 hold:

- String 6: `B:7 C:8 D:10`
- String 5: `E:7 F:8 G:10`
- String 4: `A:7 B:9 C:10`
- String 3: `D:7 E:9 F:10`
- String 2: `G:8 A:10`
- String 1: `B:7 C:8 D:10`

Three notes on most strings and two on string 2, because string 2 is tuned a major third above string 3. The window did not define the scale; the scale's notes defined what falls in the window.

A single octave of `C` major across three strings (`C D E F G A B C`):

- String 6: `C:8 D:10`
- String 5: `E:7 F:8 G:10`
- String 4: `A:7 B:9 C:10`

Two notes on the first string, then three on each of the next two. Same scale, different grouping of the same notes.

Moving to another key changes the window, not the method. `G` major is `G A B C D E F♯`. On string 4: `D:0 E:2 F♯:4 G:5 A:7 B:9 C:10 D:12`. On string 3: `G:0 A:2 B:4 C:5 D:7 E:9 F♯:11 G:12`. Compared with `C` major, only `F` became `F♯`: fret 3 becomes 4 on string 4, and fret 10 becomes 11 on string 3.

Practical habit: always name the degree as you play. In the frets 7–10 window of `C` major, the roots are `C` at string 6 fret 8, string 4 fret 10, and string 1 fret 8. The window contains every degree at least once.

## Exercises

1. Without the table, write `D` major and place it on string 4 from the open string. Expected: `D E F♯ G A B C♯ D` at frets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12`.
2. Build `G` major on string 3 from the open string. Expected: `G A B C D E F♯ G` at frets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12`.
3. Place `A` major (`A B C♯ D E F♯ G♯`) on string 5 from the open string, then on string 6 from fret 5. Expected: string 5 frets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12`; string 6 frets `5 7 9 10 12 14 16 17`.
4. From the `C` major table, list the scale notes in the window frets 2–5. Expected: string 6 `G:3 A:5`; string 5 `B:2 C:3 D:5`; string 4 `E:2 F:3 G:5`; string 3 `A:2 B:4 C:5`; string 2 `D:3 E:5`; string 1 `G:3 A:5`.
5. Play `C` major in the window frets 7–10 ascending and descending, naming each note and its degree aloud. Check: string 6 fret 8 is degree 1, string 5 fret 10 is degree 5.
6. Play one octave of `C` major on string 3 (frets `5 7 9 10 12 14 16 17`) and on string 2 (frets `1 3 5 6 8 10 12 13`). Confirm that the two octaves sound the same although the frets differ.
7. Build `F` major on string 6 from fret 1 and name the notes. Expected: frets `1 3 5 6 8 10 12 13` give `F G A B♭ C D E F`.
8. Play the first phrase of "Happy Birthday" (degrees `5 5 6 5 8 7`) in `C` major in two different regions of the neck. Expected notes: `G G A G C B`.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** Explain why a shape is a region of the full map. State where the major-third irregularity falls (strings 3–2) and what it changes.
- **Written:** Write the major scale with frets on strings 4 and 3 for `G`, `D`, and `F`.
- **Fretboard:** For a given key, locate all seven degrees across at least four strings within one four-to-five-fret window, without relying on a memorized pattern.
- **Aural:** Hear that the same scale played in two regions or on two strings has the same sound.
- **Application:** Play a short melody in two regions and name the degrees as you play.
