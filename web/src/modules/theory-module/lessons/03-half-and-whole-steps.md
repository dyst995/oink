# Half steps and whole steps

**Prerequisites:** The twelve pitch classes  
**Next:** Fretboard memorization

## Concept

The twelve pitch classes sit one **half step** apart in a circle. A half step (also called a semitone) is the smallest step in this system: move to the next class.

A **whole step** (whole tone) is two half steps: skip one class and land on the next.

On guitar, one fret = one half step. Two frets = one whole step. That is the same counting system as “adjacent keys” on a piano once you include the black keys.

Most letter pairs have a class between them, so they are a whole step apart (`C` to `D`, `F` to `G`). Two letter pairs are already adjacent: **`B`–`C`** and **`E`–`F`**. Those are half steps with no natural note between them.

Why this matters: scales are recipes written in whole and half steps. Chord formulas will later count half steps from a root. If half and whole steps are solid, those recipes are arithmetic, not mystery shapes.

## Examples

- Half step: `E` → `F`, `B` → `C`, `G` → `G♯`, `A♭` → `A`.
- Whole step: `C` → `D` (passes `C♯`/`D♭`), `F` → `G`, `A` → `B`.
- Guitar: open low `E` to fret 1 is a half step (`F`). Open `E` to fret 2 is a whole step (`F♯`).
- Counting: from `C`, one half step is `C♯`/`D♭`; two half steps (one whole step) is `D`.

## Exercises

1. Name the pitch class one half step above, and one half step below, each natural: `C D E F G A B`.
2. Mark every whole-step pair and every half-step pair among the naturals only. Confirm that only `B`–`C` and `E`–`F` are half steps.
3. On one string, play: half step up, whole step up, half step up. Name each pitch class as you go (pick any starting open string).

**Done when:** you can say whether any two neighboring letter names are a half or whole step without hesitation, and you treat one fret as one half step on the guitar.
