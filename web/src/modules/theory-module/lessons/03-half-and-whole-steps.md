## Concept

A **half step** (semitone) is the distance from one pitch class to the next in the chromatic set of twelve. It is the smallest step in ordinary Western music. A **whole step** (whole tone) is two half steps: you skip one pitch class and land on the next.

**On guitar, one fret = one half step, and two frets = one whole step.** This holds on any single string, anywhere on the neck, from the first moment you start counting. You do not need a separate rule for different frets.

Among the natural notes, most neighboring letters are a whole step apart, but two pairs are only a half step apart:

- `E` to `F`
- `B` to `C`

| Pair | Distance |
| --- | --- |
| `C` to `D` | whole step (2 frets) |
| `D` to `E` | whole step |
| `E` to `F` | half step (1 fret) |
| `F` to `G` | whole step |
| `G` to `A` | whole step |
| `A` to `B` | whole step |
| `B` to `C` | half step |

## Underlying logic

In twelve-tone equal temperament, the octave is divided into twelve steps of the same frequency ratio. Each fret on the guitar is positioned to produce one of those steps, so counting frets is counting half steps.

Be careful not to confuse **step size** with **physical distance**. Frets are physically farther apart near the nut and closer together near the body, but every fret still raises the pitch by exactly one half step. The step is a ratio of frequencies (about 5.9 percent each), not a length on the neck.

A few distinctions keep the terms precise:

- "Half step" and "semitone" count distance in pitch only. `C` to `C♯` and `C` to `D♭` are both one semitone, even though they have different interval names (later lessons).
- Counting half steps is a measuring tool, not a full description of an interval. Two different intervals can span the same number of half steps.
- A half step does not always mean a letter-name neighbor. `G` to `G♯` stays on the same letter but is still a half step.

## Musical significance

Every scale and chord formula is a recipe in whole and half steps. The major scale is built from a fixed arrangement of both. Chord shapes translate directly into counted half steps from a root. If steps are solid, formulas become arithmetic you can apply in any key, rather than patterns memorized shape by shape.

Half steps also carry strong melodic meaning in context. A note that sits a half step below a target can pull toward it (the idea of a leading tone, developed later), while a whole step usually feels less tight. This is context-dependent, not automatic.

## Guitar application

Choose any string and any fret, then move along that string only:

- String 6 fret 0 (`E`) to fret 1 (`F`) is a half step. String 6 fret 0 to fret 2 (`F♯`) is a whole step.
- String 5 fret 2 (`B`) to fret 3 (`C`) is a half step. String 5 fret 3 to fret 5 (`D`) is a whole step.
- String 1 fret 0 (`E`) to fret 1 (`F`) is a half step, the same relationship as on string 6.

Moving between strings does **not** use the one-fret rule. Because adjacent strings are tuned a fourth apart (a major third for one pair), "same fret, next string" is not a half step. You will see exactly what it is in the lesson on tuning.

Ear: play a note, then the note one fret higher, then the note two frets higher. The half step sounds like a very small move; the whole step is clearly larger but still close. Compare them back to back. Sing the first note and then your best attempt at "one half step up," and check against the guitar.

## Exercises

1. Name the pitch class one half step up and one half step down from each natural: `C D E F G A B`. **Check:** half step up gives `C♯ D♯ F F♯ G♯ A♯ C` (either enharmonic spelling is fine). Half step down gives `B C♯ D♯ E F♯ G♯ A♯` (again, either spelling is fine). Note that `E` up and `B` up land on naturals (`F` and `C`), and `F` down and `C` down do too (`E` and `B`).
2. Which two pairs of neighboring naturals are a half step apart? **Check:** `E`-`F` and `B`-`C`.
3. On string 4 (open `D`), play three notes in this order: half step up, whole step up, half step up. Name each note. **Check:** `D♯`, `F`, `F♯`, since each step is counted from the previous note.
4. From string 3 fret 0 (`G`), move up exactly three whole steps on the same string. Which fret and note do you reach? **Check:** fret 6, `C♯` or `D♭`.
5. Ear test: have a partner or a random fret generator pick a start and either 1 or 2 frets up. Play it and say "half" or "whole" without looking. Aim for 9 of 10 correct.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** You can define half step and whole step and say why one fret equals one half step.
- **Written:** You can state which natural neighbors are half steps and which are whole steps without hesitation.
- **Fretboard:** On any single string, you can move by a stated number of half or whole steps and name the resulting note.
- **Aural:** You can tell a half step from a whole step by ear on a single string in melodic playing.
- **Application:** You can explain in one sentence why "same fret on the next string" is not a half step.
