# Chord shapes as interval stacks

**Prerequisites:** Triads; seventh chords; intervals on the neck  
**Next:** Arpeggios and chord-tone targeting

## Concept

A chord **grip** is a fingering of chord tones — root, third, fifth, and maybe seventh — not a mysterious block. If you can name which finger is which tone, you can:

- keep the quality when you move the shape,
- drop or double tones on purpose,
- connect grips by voice leading (next lessons).

Movable shapes keep the same interval stack when slid up or down the neck (watch open-string chords that are not fully movable). The root’s fret names the chord; the stack names the quality.

## Examples

- An E-shape major barre: root on low E, major third and fifth in the grip — sliding to fret 3 yields G major if the root lands on `G`.
- A small triad on the D–G–B strings: identify root vs third vs fifth by interval from the lowest chord tone you hear as root.
- `C7` shape: find the `B♭` (♭7) in the grip; remove it and you are left closer to a C triad.

## Exercises

1. Play any major shape you know. Point to root, third, and fifth. Mute one string at a time and say which tone disappeared.
2. Move that shape up two frets. Name the new chord. Confirm the quality is unchanged.
3. Play a dominant 7 shape. Locate the ♭7. Lower or raise that tone by a fret and name what chord quality you accidentally created (or why it broke the chord).

**Done when:** you can name R / 3 / 5 (and 7 if present) inside a shape and transpose the shape without losing the quality.
