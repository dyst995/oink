# Intervals in music

**Prerequisites:** Interval names and quality  
**Next:** Triads

## Concept

Intervals are not only labels. They are how notes **relate** in time and in vertical stacks.

**Melodic** intervals are heard one after another (a line). **Harmonic** intervals are heard together (a chord or double-stop). Same named distance either way; the musical job differs.

**Consonance** and **dissonance** describe tendency, not moral value. In tonal music:

- Stable, “at rest” harmonic intervals include perfect unisons/octaves, perfect fifths, and (in common practice) major/minor thirds and sixths.
- Seconds, sevenths, and the tritone (A4/d5) tend to feel unstable and want to move.

That is why chords are built from stacked **thirds** sitting under a **fifth** from the root: those intervals fuse into a clear color (major vs minor) while still sounding like one harmony. Seconds packed into a chord usually sound like tension or a mistake unless you want that effect.

**Inversion** (practical level): flip the lower and upper notes of an interval so the old top becomes the bottom. A useful check: a simple interval and its inversion add to nine (2nd ↔ 7th, 3rd ↔ 6th, 4th ↔ 5th), and quality swaps in pairs (major ↔ minor, perfect stays perfect, augmented ↔ diminished). Example: `C` up to `E` (M3) inverts to `E` up to `C` (m6).

On guitar, the same named interval appears as different fingerings (one string vs across strings). The name is the theory; the shape is geography. Prefer knowing the name and half-step count, then finding more than one way to play it.

## Examples

- Melody: `G`–`A`–`B` is M2 then M2. Harmony: `G` and `B` together is a M3 — the sound of a major triad’s bottom.
- `G` and `D` together: P5 — hollow, stable; add `B` and you hear G major.
- Tritone `F`–`B`: unstable; in C major it pulls toward `E`–`C` or similar resolutions — preview of dominant tension.
- Invert `A` up to `E` (P5) → `E` up to `A` (P4).

## Exercises

1. Play each as a melody, then as a double-stop (or two notes ringing): m3, M3, P5 above open `A`. Describe which feel more settled.
2. Pick `C`–`A` (M6). Invert it and name the result. Check on the neck in two places.
3. In any major key you know, find degrees `1`–`3`–`5`. Name the intervals `1→3`, `3→5`, and `1→5`. Those three distances are the skeleton of the next unit’s triads.

**Done when:** you can tell melodic from harmonic use, invert a simple interval and name it, and explain why thirds and fifths are the usual building blocks of chords.
