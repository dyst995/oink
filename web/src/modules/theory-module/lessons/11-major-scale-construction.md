# Major scale construction

**Prerequisites:** Half steps and whole steps; twelve pitch classes  
**Next:** Scale degrees and formulas

## Concept

A **major scale** is seven pitch classes built from a root by a fixed step recipe:

**W – W – H – W – W – W – H**

W = whole step (2 half steps), H = half step (1). The eighth note returns to the root one octave higher. The pattern spans 12 half steps: 2+2+1+2+2+2+1.

Two rules are applied together, and both are required:

1. **Distance:** each note must sit the correct number of half steps from the previous one.
2. **Letter:** each degree uses the **next letter** of the musical alphabet (`A B C D E F G`, then back to `A`), so a scale has seven different letters, each once.

Distance picks the sound. Letter picks the spelling. A scale that gets the sound right but repeats a letter is misspelled.

## Underlying logic

Seven notes and seven letters is why one letter per degree works. When you need a note that is not natural, you adjust the letter with `♯` or `♭` rather than switching letters.

Example: the major scale from `F♯` must contain an `E♯`, not an `F`. The note you play is the same fret as `F` natural, but the scale already used the letter `F` for the root, so degree 7 takes the letter `E`.

The recipe splits into two identical halves: **W W H** + **W** + **W W H**. Each half is a four-note group called a **tetrachord**. The whole step in the middle joins them. This split is the reason keys are related by fifths (next lessons).

Where the half steps fall matters: they sit between degrees `3–4` and `7–8`. The natural notes have half steps at `B–C` and `E–F`, which is why `C` major needs no accidentals: its half steps already fall at `E–F` (3–4) and `B–C` (7–8).

## Musical significance

The two half steps give the scale its pull. Degree 7 sits one half step under the tonic and leans up into it. Degree 4 leans down toward 3. The tonic feels like home because every other note has a defined tendency toward it or around it.

Every major key uses this same internal pattern. Changing the root moves the whole sound up or down without changing its character.

## Guitar application

On one string, W = two frets and H = one fret. Nothing else is needed to build a scale.

- String 6 from open `E`: frets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12` give `E F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯ E`.
- String 5 from `C` (fret 3): frets `3 5 7 8 10 12 14 15` give `C D E F G A B C`.
- String 4 from open `D`: frets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12` give `D E F♯ G A B C♯ D`.

The fret numbers for a major scale starting at a root fret `r` on any one string are always `r, r+2, r+4, r+5, r+7, r+9, r+11, r+12`.

Spelling is still your job. The fret `4` on string 6 is `G♯` in E major but would be `A♭` in a flat key. Fret numbers cannot tell you the letter; the letter rule does.

| Root | Scale (letters correct) | Accidentals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `C` | `C D E F G A B` | none |
| `G` | `G A B C D E F♯` | `F♯` |
| `D` | `D E F♯ G A B C♯` | `F♯ C♯` |
| `A` | `A B C♯ D E F♯ G♯` | `C♯ F♯ G♯` |
| `E` | `E F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯` | four sharps |
| `F` | `F G A B♭ C D E` | `B♭` |
| `B♭` | `B♭ C D E♭ F G A` | `B♭ E♭` |
| `E♭` | `E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C D` | `E♭ A♭ B♭` |
| `F♯` | `F♯ G♯ A♯ B C♯ D♯ E♯` | six sharps, including `E♯` |

Notice that `F` needs `B♭` (not `A♯`): after `A` comes the letter `B`. The two tetrachords are `F G A B♭` (W W H) and `C D E F` (W W H), joined by the whole step `B♭–C`.

## Exercises

1. Build the major scale from `D` using only W/H. Expected: `D E F♯ G A B C♯`. Check that each letter appears once.
2. Build `B♭` major. Expected: `B♭ C D E♭ F G A`. Confirm you did not write `A♯` or repeat any letter.
3. Build `A♭` major. Expected: `A♭ B♭ C D♭ E♭ F G`.
4. Build `F♯` major and explain in one sentence why degree 7 is `E♯`, not `F`. Expected: `F♯ G♯ A♯ B C♯ D♯ E♯`.
5. Find the error: `G A B C D E G♭`. Why is it wrong, and what is the correct degree 7? Expected: the letter `F` is missing and `G` repeats; degree 7 is `F♯`.
6. On string 5, start at fret 5 (`D`) and play the recipe by fret count only. Name the notes afterward. Expected: frets `5 7 9 10 12 14 16 17` give `D E F♯ G A B C♯ D`.
7. Play the same recipe starting on the open B string (string 2). Hear the character, then name the notes. Expected: frets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12` give `B C♯ D♯ E F♯ G♯ A♯ B`.
8. Sing or hum the scale along with your playing, then play only degree 7 and resolve it up to the root. Describe the pull you hear.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** State the recipe as steps and as fret counts on one string. Explain the two rules (distance and letter) and why both are needed.
- **Written:** Spell the major scale from any root in `C G D A E F B♭ E♭ A♭`, and from `F♯`, with correct letters and no repeated letters.
- **Fretboard:** Play a major scale along one string from any fret, naming each pitch class as you go.
- **Aural:** Hear the half step between degrees 7 and 8 and identify it as the strongest pull in the scale.
- **Application:** Explain why `F♯` major contains `E♯` and why `F` major contains `B♭`.
