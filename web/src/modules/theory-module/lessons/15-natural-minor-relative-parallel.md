# Natural minor: relative and parallel

**Prerequisites:** The major scale across the neck  
**Next:** Harmonic and melodic minor

## Concept

The **natural minor scale** has the step pattern:

**W – H – W – W – H – W – W**

and the formula, against the major scale on the same root:

`1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7`

Three degrees are lowered compared with major: the third, sixth, and seventh. That is the whole difference in sound. The lowered third gives the minor triad `1 ♭3 5`.

Two relationships connect major and minor:

- **Relative:** a major key and a minor key sharing the same key signature and the same seven notes. `C` major and `A` minor are relatives.
- **Parallel:** a major and a minor with the same tonic. `C` major and `C` minor are parallels. They have different notes.

## Underlying logic

Take the notes of any major scale and start on degree 6 instead of degree 1. For `C` major, degree 6 is `A`: `A B C D E F G A`. The step pattern from `A` is W H W W H W W, the natural minor pattern. So the **relative minor starts on degree 6 of its major** (equivalently, a minor third below the major tonic).

Same notes, but a different tonic. The music does not become minor because the notes changed; it becomes minor because `A` is now treated as home. A tune in `A` minor that uses only the white keys is not "C major in disguise": the pull is toward `A`, the chord `Am` is home, and `C` is a supporting chord.

The parallel relationship is a different comparison. Same tonic, different third, sixth, and seventh:

| Degree | `C` major | `C` natural minor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | `E` | `E♭` |
| 6 | `A` | `A♭` |
| 7 | `B` | `B♭` |

So `C` minor needs three flats (`B♭ E♭ A♭`): the signature of `E♭` major, its relative.

Finding the relative minor from a major tonic: go down three half steps (and spell by letter: down a third). Finding the relative major from a minor tonic: go up three half steps.

| Major key | Relative minor | Signature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `C` | `A` | none |
| `G` | `E` | `F♯` |
| `D` | `B` | `F♯ C♯` |
| `A` | `F♯` | `F♯ C♯ G♯` |
| `F` | `D` | `B♭` |
| `B♭` | `G` | `B♭ E♭` |
| `E♭` | `C` | `B♭ E♭ A♭` |

## Musical significance

Minor is not a darker copy of major with the same behavior. In natural minor, degree 7 is a whole step below the tonic, which weakens the pull of the dominant (see next lesson). The `♭6` and `♭7` give a soft, open, sometimes modal quality. Many folk, rock, and blues-derived tunes use natural minor with no raised degree.

The relative relationship explains why songs move between `C` and `Am`, or `G` and `Em`, so easily: they share all notes, so only the tonic shifts.

The parallel relationship explains borrowing: a `C` major song may use `Fm` or `A♭` or `B♭` from `C` minor for color. That is a deliberate change of mood.

## Guitar application

On string 6 from open `E`, natural minor is frets `0 2 3 5 7 8 10 12`: `E F♯ G A B C D E`. Compare to `E` major on the same string: frets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12`. The differences fall at degree 3 (fret 4 → 3), degree 6 (9 → 8), degree 7 (11 → 10).

On string 5 from open `A`: frets `0 2 3 5 7 8 10 12` give `A B C D E F G A`. Same fret pattern as `E` minor on string 6, because both strings are tuned the same distance from their roots.

Relative minor on the neck: from a major root at fret `r` on string 6, the relative minor root is on the same string at fret `r−3`, or equivalently an octave up. From `C` at string 6, fret 8, the `A` is at fret 5 on the same string. Their scales share notes: on string 6, `A` minor frets `5 7 8 10 12 13 15 17` give `A B C D E F G A`, and `C` major frets `8 10 12 13 15 17 19 20` give the same letters starting from `C`.

Natural minor triads in `A` minor (stacked thirds from each degree): `Am` (`A C E`), `B°` (`B D F`), `C` (`C E G`), `Dm` (`D F A`), `Em` (`E G B`), `F` (`F A C`), `G` (`G B D`). Note that the chord on degree 5 is minor (`Em`, written `v`), not major. This is the natural minor chord set, previewed here and developed in the diatonic triads lesson.

Playing tip: when you play `A` minor over `Am`, target `A`, `C`, `E`. The note `C` (degree `♭3`) is what makes the chord minor. If you land on `C♯` the sound changes immediately to major.

## Exercises

1. Write the natural minor scale of `E`, `B`, `D`, `G`, and `C♯` using the formula `1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7`. Expected: `E F♯ G A B C D`; `B C♯ D E F♯ G A`; `D E F G A B♭ C`; `G A B♭ C D E♭ F`; `C♯ D♯ E F♯ G♯ A B`.
2. Name the relative minor of `D`, `F`, `A`, `B♭`, and `E♭` major. Expected: `B`, `D`, `F♯`, `G`, `C`.
3. Name the relative major of `E`, `F♯`, `D`, `G`, and `C` minor. Expected: `G`, `A`, `F`, `B♭`, `E♭`.
4. Write `D` major and `D` minor side by side. List the three changing notes. Expected: `F♯→F`, `B→B♭`, `C♯→C`.
5. On string 6, play `E` natural minor from the open string, then `G` major from fret 3. Both are the same pitch classes except for the starting note. Expected: `E F♯ G A B C D` and `G A B C D E F♯`.
6. Play `Am` open (`x02210`), then play the `A` natural minor scale on string 5. Land on `C` (string 5, fret 3) and hear that it is the minor third. Raise it by one fret to `C♯` and hear the shift to major.
7. Compare by ear: play `C` major then `C` minor scale on string 5 from fret 3. Describe the difference in a word or two. Check frets: major `3 5 7 8 10 12 14 15`; minor `3 5 6 8 10 11 13 15`.
8. Play `Am – F – C – G` and say whether the tonic feels like `A` or `C`. Then play `C – G – Am – F` and say again. Discuss what changed (order and where you stop).

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** Distinguish relative and parallel precisely. Explain why `A` minor and `C` major are different keys although they share notes.
- **Written:** Spell natural minor in at least `A E B D G C F♯` from the formula, and find any relative pair in under five seconds.
- **Fretboard:** Play natural minor on string 6 and string 5 from a chosen root, then find the relative major root three frets up on the same string.
- **Aural:** Hear `3` versus `♭3` over a root, and identify a tune as minor from its tonic.
- **Application:** Compare parallel major and minor on the same root and state the three changing degrees.
