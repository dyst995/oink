# Harmonic and melodic minor

**Prerequisites:** Natural minor, relative and parallel  
**Next:** Pentatonic and blues scales

## Concept

Natural minor has `♭7`. Two common variants raise degrees to solve specific problems:

| Scale | Formula | Changed from natural minor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural minor | `1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7` | none |
| Harmonic minor | `1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 7` | raised 7 |
| Melodic minor (ascending) | `1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 7` | raised 6 and 7 |

In `A` minor:

- Natural: `A B C D E F G`
- Harmonic: `A B C D E F G♯`
- Melodic ascending: `A B C D E F♯ G♯`

These are not different keys. The key signature stays natural minor (`A` minor has none). The raised notes appear as accidentals when the music needs them.

## Underlying logic

**Why raise 7.** In natural minor, degree 7 is a whole step below the tonic (`G` to `A`). The chord built on degree 5 is minor (`Em`), and `Em` has no note a half step below `A`, so the pull back to `A` is weak. Raise `G` to `G♯` and two things happen:

- The V chord becomes major (`E G♯ B`), a stronger dominant.
- Degree 7 sits a half step below the tonic and leads into it. In major this note is called the leading tone; the raise gives minor the same function.

That is harmonic minor: natural minor with a raised 7, shaped by harmony. The chords `E` and `E7` come from it.

**The cost.** Raising only the 7 leaves `F` to `G♯`, three half steps (an **augmented second**). That gap is awkward to sing and has a distinctive, exotic sound.

**Why raise 6 as well.** Raise the sixth too and the gap closes: `F♯` to `G♯` is a whole step, and the line `E F♯ G♯ A` is smooth and stepwise. That ascending form is melodic minor, a melodic fix. In classical practice it is used going up and returns to natural minor going down (`A G F E`), because a descending line does not need the leading tone. Jazz and many modern players use the ascending form both ways; that is vocabulary choice, not a rule of minor keys.

None of this means "minor uses harmonic minor". A raised 7, or raised 6 and 7, is used where the music needs to lean into the tonic.

## Musical significance

Cadences call for the raised 7. In `Am – Dm – E – Am` the `E` is major because its `G♯` leads to `A`. Many minor-key songs mix natural-minor chords with a major V for this reason.

The augmented second between `♭6` and `7` is often heard as dramatic, classical, or Middle-Eastern in flavor. The raised-6-and-7 melodic minor gives a smooth, bright-minor sound, used in jazz lines over `Am(maj7)`.

## Guitar application

On string 5 from open `A`:

- Natural minor: frets `0 2 3 5 7 8 10 12`
- Harmonic minor: frets `0 2 3 5 7 8 11 12`. The gap from fret 8 (`F`) to fret 11 (`G♯`) is three frets, the augmented second.
- Melodic minor ascending: frets `0 2 3 5 7 9 11 12`, giving `A B C D E F♯ G♯ A`.

Fret distance reads the intervals directly: whole step = 2 frets, half step = 1, augmented second = 3.

The leading tone in a cadence, with open chords:

- `E7` (`020100`) goes to `Am` (`x02210`). On string 3, fret 1 is `G♯` and fret 2 is `A`: the leading tone resolves up a half step.
- Strings 2 and 1 are open `B` and `E` in both chords. These common tones hold still while string 3 moves.

The same idea in `D` minor: `A7` (`x02020`) has `C♯` on string 2, fret 2. It resolves to `Dm` (`xx0231`), where string 2, fret 3 is `D`. That is `C♯→D`, the leading tone of `D` minor.

Use the raised note where it has a job. Held against a plain `Am` chord, `G♯` clashes with the root `A` (it is a major seventh above it). Use it as a leading tone, a passing tone, or over `E` or `E7`, where it is the chord's third. If you want the major-seventh color against `Am`, you are choosing an `Am(maj7)` sound deliberately.

## Exercises

1. Write natural, harmonic, and ascending melodic minor for `E`. Expected: natural `E F♯ G A B C D`; harmonic `E F♯ G A B C D♯`; melodic `E F♯ G A B C♯ D♯`.
2. Write harmonic minor for `D`. Expected: `D E F G A B♭ C♯`. Name the augmented second. Expected: `B♭` to `C♯`.
3. Spell the V chord of `A` minor, `D` minor, and `E` minor using harmonic minor. Expected: `E G♯ B`, `A C♯ E`, `B D♯ F♯`.
4. On string 5, play `A` harmonic minor from the open string. Count the frets from fret 8 to fret 11 and call it the augmented second. Expected frets: `0 2 3 5 7 8 11 12`.
5. On string 5, play `A` melodic minor ascending (`0 2 3 5 7 9 11 12`), then natural minor descending (`12 10 8 7 5 3 2 0`).
6. Play `E7` then `Am` open chords. Listen to string 3 move `G♯→A`. Then play `Em` (`022000`) then `Am`. Compare the strength of the two arrivals.
7. Play `Am – Dm – E7 – Am` slowly. In `E7`, locate `G♯` (string 3, fret 1) and in the final `Am` the `A` it moves to (string 3, fret 2). Hum `G♯ → A` over the change.
8. Write a four-bar melody in `A` minor whose last two notes are `G♯ A` over an `E` chord resolving to `Am`. Mark each raised note and say which scale it comes from. Expected: the `G♯` comes from harmonic minor.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** Explain why 7 is raised, and why 6 is raised too in melodic minor. Describe the augmented second and where it occurs.
- **Written:** Spell harmonic and melodic minor in at least `A E D G B`, and spell the V chord of those keys.
- **Fretboard:** Play harmonic minor on one string and point out the three-fret gap. Play the leading-tone resolution `G♯→A` on string 3.
- **Aural:** Hear a leading tone resolving up by half step. Distinguish `Am – Em – Am` from `Am – E – Am`.
- **Application:** Use a major V chord in a minor progression, and explain why the key signature does not change.
