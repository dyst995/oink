# Diatonic harmony

**Prerequisites:** Major scale; triads; seventh chords  
**Next:** Functional progressions

## Concept

**Diatonic harmony** means chords built using only the notes of one key's scale. If you stack thirds on each degree of a major scale and stay inside the scale, you get seven triads and seven seventh chords. Naming them with **Roman numerals** lets you describe a progression independently of key.

Roman numeral conventions used here:

- Uppercase for major triads (`I`, `IV`, `V`) and lowercase for minor triads (`ii`, `iii`, `vi`).
- `°` for diminished triads (`vii°`) and `ø` for half-diminished sevenths (`viiø7`).
- Add `7` or `maj7` for sevenths (`V7`, `Imaj7`, `ii7`).

## Underlying logic

### Building chords from the scale

Take the scale of `C` major: `C D E F G A B`. For each degree, take that note, skip one scale note, take the next, skip one, take the next (and skip one more for the seventh). Because the major scale mixes whole and half steps, the thirds you collect are sometimes major and sometimes minor, which creates different qualities.

| Degree | Triad (C major) | Seventh chord (C major) | Roman numeral (triad) | Roman numeral (seventh) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `C` | `Cmaj7` | `I` | `Imaj7` |
| 2 | `Dm` | `Dm7` | `ii` | `ii7` |
| 3 | `Em` | `Em7` | `iii` | `iii7` |
| 4 | `F` | `Fmaj7` | `IV` | `IVmaj7` |
| 5 | `G` | `G7` | `V` | `V7` |
| 6 | `Am` | `Am7` | `vi` | `vi7` |
| 7 | `B°` | `Bm7♭5` | `vii°` | `viiø7` |

The qualities follow the same pattern in **every** major key: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished for triads; maj7, m7, m7, maj7, 7, m7, m7♭5 for sevenths. Only the root names change. In `G` major: `G Am Bm C D Em F♯°`. As sevenths: `Gmaj7 Am7 Bm7 Cmaj7 D7 Em7 F♯m7♭5`.

### Minor keys: a first overview

Minor harmony needs more care because the minor scale has more than one common form.

**Natural minor** (`A` minor: `A B C D E F G`) gives:

| Degree | Triad | Seventh | Roman numeral |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `Am` | `Am7` | `i` |
| 2 | `B°` | `Bm7♭5` | `ii°` |
| 3 | `C` | `Cmaj7` | `III` |
| 4 | `Dm` | `Dm7` | `iv` |
| 5 | `Em` | `Em7` | `v` |
| 6 | `F` | `Fmaj7` | `VI` |
| 7 | `G` | `G7` | `VII` |

**Harmonic minor** raises degree 7 (`G` becomes `G♯`). The chord on degree 5 becomes `E` or `E7` (major or dominant), which restores a leading tone and a strong `V` to `i`. The chord on degree 7 becomes `G♯°` or `G♯dim7`. This is why `i iv V7 i` (`Am Dm E7 Am`) is a typical minor-key cadence even though pure natural minor would give `Em`.

Do not memorise minor as one fixed list. It is a family: natural minor supplies the stable diatonic set, harmonic minor supplies the major `V`, and melodic-minor ideas appear in melody and some chords. Lesson 30 returns to this when we borrow chords between major and minor.

### Same quality does not mean same function

`G7` appears in the table for `C` major (as `V7`) and for `A` natural minor (as `VII7`, a major triad on degree 7 with an added minor seventh). They are the same chord with the same notes. In `C` major it is the dominant and pulls toward `C`. In `A` natural minor, the same chord is a `VII7`; its natural pull is toward `C`, which is `III` in that key, and not toward the tonic `Am`. Same notes, different job in the key. **A chord with a dominant-seventh sound is not automatically the dominant chord of the key you are in.**

## Musical significance

Diatonic chords form the pool for most songs in a key. Learn the seven chords of a key and you can predict and construct many progressions. The Roman numerals also let you hear relationships: a `I IV V` in `E` major sounds like `I IV V` in `A` major; only the pitch level changes.

## Guitar application

Chord shapes for `C` major in open position (frets 6 to 1):

| Numeral | Triad | Frets | Seventh | Frets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `I` | `C` | `x32010` | `Cmaj7` | `x32000` |
| `ii` | `Dm` | `xx0231` | `Dm7` | `xx0211` |
| `iii` | `Em` | `022000` | `Em7` | `022030` |
| `IV` | `F` | `xx3211` | `Fmaj7` | `xx3210` |
| `V` | `G` | `320003` | `G7` | `320001` |
| `vi` | `Am` | `x02210` | `Am7` | `x02010` |
| `vii°` | `B°` | `x2343x` | `Bm7♭5` | `x2323x` |

To play any progression in a new key, keep the shapes you know and change the key, or use movable barre shapes (`E`-shape on string 6, `A`-shape on string 5). A progression's numerals tell you the roots; the quality (uppercase or lowercase) tells you major or minor.

Finding the diatonic chords on the neck: take any root on string 6 or 5 and look up the fret of the next root (a second, third, and so on above it). For example, in `G` major with root at string 6 fret 3, the next scale note `A` is at fret 5 and has the quality `m`, so `ii` is `Am` or `Am7` at fret 5, `iii` is `Bm` at fret 7, and `IV` is `C` at fret 8. That shows you chords as a **path along one string**, a useful navigation habit.

## Exercises

1. Write the diatonic triads and seventh chords of `D` major and `B♭` major, with Roman numerals. Answer key: `D Em F♯m G A Bm C♯°` and `Dmaj7 Em7 F♯m7 Gmaj7 A7 Bm7 C♯m7♭5`; `B♭ Cm Dm E♭ F Gm A°` and `B♭maj7 Cm7 Dm7 E♭maj7 F7 Gm7 Am7♭5`.
2. Write the diatonic chords of `A` natural minor with numerals; then change `E` to `E7` and note which scale note is now outside natural minor (`G♯`).
3. On guitar, play `Cmaj7 Am7 Dm7 G7` (`I vi ii V` in `C`). Then play `I vi ii V` in `G` using the table's `G` major chords: `Gmaj7 Em7 Am7 D7`.
4. Choose any key and write the numerals for `Am Dm E7 Am` in `A` minor (`i iv V7 i`). Say which note of `E7` is not in `A` natural minor.
5. Name `G7` as a numeral in `C` major (`V7`) and in `A` natural minor (`VII7`). Play `G7` to `C` and `G7` to `Am`. Explain which one sounds like a convincing resolution and why the numerals differ even though the chord is the same.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** explain how stacking thirds within a scale gives chord qualities, and why the quality pattern is identical in all major keys.
- **Written:** produce the seven diatonic triads and seventh chords, with numerals, for any major key through four sharps or flats.
- **Written:** write the diatonic chords of natural minor and explain what harmonic minor changes.
- **Fretboard:** play at least one voicing of each diatonic chord in `C` and one other key without a chart.
- **Aural:** hear `I`, `IV`, `V`, and `vi` in a familiar key and name the numerals by ear.
- **Application:** explain why `V7` in major and `VII7` in natural minor have the same chord quality and different function.
