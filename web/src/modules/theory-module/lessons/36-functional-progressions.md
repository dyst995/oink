# Functional progressions

**Prerequisites:** Diatonic harmony; cadences  
**Next:** Secondary dominants

## Concept

Chords in a key do different jobs. We sort those jobs into three **functions**:

- **Tonic (T):** home, rest, arrival. Typically `I`; `vi` often acts as a substitute; `iii` is a weaker tonic substitute.
- **Predominant (PD):** departure, motion away from home, preparation. Typically `IV` and `ii`.
- **Dominant (D):** tension that wants to return home. Typically `V`, `V7`, and `vii°`.

The classic pattern is **T to PD to D to T**. It is a tendency, not a law: popular music, blues, and modal writing often use other orders. Functions describe what a chord tends to do in a key, not what the chord is made of.

## Underlying logic

### Why these chords group together

Chords share function when they share notes and pull in similar directions in the scale:

- `ii` (`D F A` in `C`) and `IV` (`F A C`) share `F` and `A`, and both contain scale degree 4 (`F`), which wants to fall to `E`.
- `V` (`G B D`) and `vii°` (`B D F`) share `B` and `D`. Both contain degree 7 (`B`), which wants to rise to `C`.
- `I` (`C E G`) and `vi` (`A C E`) share `C` and `E`, so `vi` can stand in for `I` without a large change in sound.

### Core progressions

**I IV V I** in `C`: `C F G C`. Tonic, predominant, dominant, tonic.

**ii V I** in `C` major: `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7`. Roots move down by a fifth each time. Guide tones: the seventh of `Dm7` (`C`) falls a half step to the third of `G7` (`B`). The third of `Dm7` (`F`) is the same note as the seventh of `G7` (`F`), so it holds, then falls a half step to the third of `Cmaj7` (`E`). The `B` (third of `G7`) is also the major seventh of `Cmaj7`, so it can stay in place across that last change.

**Minor ii V i** in `C` minor: `Dm7♭5 G7 Cm7` (the `G7` carries `B`, borrowed from harmonic minor).

**12-bar blues** (one common form, with seventh chords throughout):

| Bar | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chord | `I7` | `I7` or `IV7` | `I7` | `I7` |

| Bar | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chord | `IV7` | `IV7` | `I7` | `I7` |

| Bar | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chord | `V7` | `IV7` | `I7` | `I7` or `V7` |

In blues, `I7` and `IV7` are **dominant-quality chords that are not functioning as dominants of the key**: neither is a tension waiting for a resolution. That is why "a 7 chord is always V" is a misleading shortcut. The `V7` in bars 9 and 12 does lead back to `I7`.

### Movement is not always "forward"

- `V` to `IV` (a **retrogression**) is common in rock and blues and sounds like a deliberate step away from the dominant, not a mistake.
- `V` to `vi` is a **deceptive** move: dominant expectation, tonic-substitute arrival.
- `IV` to `I` is a gentle **plagal** close.

## Musical significance

Functional thinking explains why familiar progressions feel complete. It also lets you predict. After `ii` and `V`, you expect `I`; when you hear `vi` instead, the surprise is audible because you had a path in mind.

### Patterns you will meet

These are generic patterns. Many songs use them, and the lesson only describes the patterns, not any particular piece.

| Pattern | Example in `G` | Numeral shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Four-chord loop | `G D Em C` | `I V vi IV` |
| Doo-wop-style cycle | `G Em C D` | `I vi IV V` |
| Jazz turnaround | `Gmaj7 Em7 Am7 D7` | `I vi ii V` |
| Rock cadence | `G F C G` | `I ♭VII IV I` (borrowed chord; see modal interchange) |

## Guitar application

In `G`, with open-position shapes (strings 6 to 1):

- `I IV V I`: `G` (`320003`), `C` (`x32010`), `D` (`xx0232`), `G`.
- `I vi IV V`: `G`, `Em` (`022000`), `C`, `D`.
- `ii V I` in `G`: `Am7` (`x02010`), `D7` (`xx0212`), `Gmaj7` (`320002`).

Shell voicings for `ii V I` in `C` (strings 6 to 1):

- `Dm7`: `x5x56x` (`D C F`).
- `G7`: `3xx46x` (`G B F`; here the `B` and `F` are the upper two notes).
- `Cmaj7`: `x3x45x` (`C B E`).

The upper two notes move `5/6` to `4/6` to `4/5` (strings 3 and 2): `C F`, then `B F`, then `B E`. The two guide tones travel at most one fret each time.

For the blues in `A`, use `A7` (`x02020`), `D7` (`xx0212`), `E7` (`020100`) in the shape above.

## Exercises

1. Label each chord's function (T, PD, D) in `C F G C`, `C Am Dm G`, and `C Em F G`. Answer key: `T PD D T`; `T T PD D`; `T T PD D` (`iii` is a weak tonic here).
2. Write `ii V I` in `G`, `D`, and `F` major. Answers: `Am7 D7 Gmaj7`; `Em7 A7 Dmaj7`; `Gm7 C7 Fmaj7`.
3. Write the 12-bar blues in `A` with seventh chords, placing chords in bars 1 to 12. Answer: `A7 A7 A7 A7 | D7 D7 A7 A7 | E7 D7 A7 E7`.
4. Play `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7` using the shell voicings above and name the guide tones that move by half step.
5. Play `C F G C` and then `C F G F`. Describe, in terms of function, what changed in how the phrase ends.
6. Take any four-chord loop above and say whether it ends with a dominant-to-tonic resolution.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** explain T, PD, and D as functions and say why function depends on context and not only on chord quality.
- **Conceptual:** explain why `I7` and `IV7` in a blues are not dominants of the key.
- **Written:** write `I IV V`, `ii V I`, and the 12-bar blues in any key with chord names.
- **Fretboard:** play `ii V I` in two keys with movable shells and identify the moving guide tones.
- **Aural:** distinguish a resolution (`V` to `I`) from a deceptive cadence (`V` to `vi`) by ear.
- **Application:** write a four-bar progression with function labels and justify each chord choice.
