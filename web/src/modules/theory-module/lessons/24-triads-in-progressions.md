# Triads in progressions

**Prerequisites:** Cadences; triad voice leading  
**Next:** CAGED as navigation

## Concept

A triad is a tool for **three jobs** in a progression:

- **Comping:** short triad voicings on the upper strings, in rhythm, over a bass note.
- **Outlining:** playing the chord's notes one at a time (an arpeggio) so a single line states the harmony.
- **Improvising:** using triad tones as the strong, stable targets and scale notes as the connections.

All three use the same material: the root, third, and fifth of the current chord, chosen so the move to the next chord is smooth (previous lesson).

## Underlying logic

**Chord tones are the strong notes.** At any moment, root, third, and fifth agree with the chord. Other scale notes pass between them. If the strong beats hold chord tones and the weak beats hold passing notes, the line follows the harmony; if they are reversed, the line sounds like it ignores the chords.

**The third is the most informative.** It decides major or minor. A line that lands on the third of each chord makes the changes audible. Many lines that "sound like the changes" simply hit thirds on strong beats.

**Common tones are anchors.** Notes shared by neighbouring chords can be held while others move. In `G – Em – C – D` (`I – vi – IV – V` in `G`):

| Chord | Numeral | Root | Third | Fifth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `G` | `I` | `G` | `B` | `D` |
| `Em` | `vi` | `E` | `G` | `B` |
| `C` | `IV` | `C` | `E` | `G` |
| `D` | `V` | `D` | `F♯` | `A` |

`G` appears in `G`, `Em`, and `C`. `B` appears in `G` and `Em`. Only `D` contains `F♯`, the leading tone of the key. Seeing this, you know which notes can stay and which must move.

**Approach and passing tones.** A half step below or above a target leads into it. To reach `G` (a chord tone), approach from `F♯` or `A`. A scale tone between two chord tones (such as `C` between `B` and `D`) is a passing tone on a weak beat.

## Musical significance

Rhythm guitar, riffs, and solos are the same harmony viewed three ways. A player who reads chords as triads can:

- keep the sound light and clear on the top three strings while a bassist covers the root;
- state the harmony in a solo without playing the chord;
- reharmonize lightly: an `Em` triad over a `C` bass gives `C E G B`, the notes of `Cmaj7` (a preview of the next lesson), because the triad changes meaning with the bass under it.

## Guitar application

**Comping with triads on strings 3–2–1.** A smooth path for `G – Em – C – D` that uses the common tones:

| Chord | Frets (str 3, 2, 1) | Notes low to high | Motion from previous |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `G` | `7 8 7` | `D G B` | start |
| `Em` | `9 8 7` | `E G B` | `D→E` up 2; `G` and `B` stay |
| `C` | `9 8 8` | `E G C` | `B→C` up 1; `E` and `G` stay |
| `D` | `7 7 5` | `D F♯ A` | `E→D` 2, `G→F♯` 1, `C→A` 3 (down) |
| `G` | `7 8 7` | `D G B` | `D` stays; `F♯→G` up 1; `A→B` up 2 |

Motion per change: 2, 1, 6, 3. The hand stays within frets 5–9.

Add the bass on the lower strings to complete the harmony. Roots on the lower strings:

| Chord | Bass root | String and fret |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `G` | `G` | string 6, fret 3 |
| `Em` | `E` | string 6, open |
| `C` | `C` | string 5, fret 3 |
| `D` | `D` | string 4, open |

A comping pattern in 4/4, counting eighths, one chord per bar:

- Beat 1: bass root, alone.
- The "and" of 2: the three-string triad, short.
- Beat 3: bass root or its fifth.
- The "and" of 4: the triad again.

Hold the shape at the same position and let only the changing strings move.

**Outlining with arpeggios.** Quarter notes, one chord per bar, using the same strings (string and fret):

| Chord | Quarter notes (string:fret) | Note names |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `G` | `3:7 2:8 1:7 2:8` | `D G B G` |
| `Em` | `3:9 2:8 1:7 2:8` | `E G B G` |
| `C` | `3:9 2:8 1:8 2:8` | `E G C G` |
| `D` | `3:7 2:7 1:5 2:7` | `D F♯ A F♯` |

The `G` is a held-over note across the first three bars, and bar 4 moves it to `F♯`, the leading tone. The line is the progression.

**Improvising with triad tones.** Build up in three steps over the same four chords.

1. **Chord tones only.** One note per beat, only root, third, or fifth of the current chord. Land on the third on beat 1 of each bar.
2. **Add passing tones.** Place scale notes of `G` major (`G A B C D E F♯`) on weak beats between chord tones. Example over `C`: `E` (beat 1), `D` (beat 2, passing), `C` (beat 3), `E` (beat 4).
3. **Add approach notes.** A half step below the target on the beat before: over `D`, play `F` (a half step below `F♯`) on beat 4 of the previous bar, then land on `F♯`.

What to avoid: holding `C` over the `G` chord. It sits a half step above the chord's `B`, so it clashes if you stay on it. As a short passing tone it is fine, and over the `C` chord it is the root.

## Exercises

1. Write the triad tones of `G Em C D` and of `Am F C G`. Expected: `G B D`, `E G B`, `C E G`, `D F♯ A`; `A C E`, `F A C`, `C E G`, `G B D`.
2. For each chord in `G – Em – C – D`, name which notes are shared with the next chord. Expected: `G` to `Em`: `G B`; `Em` to `C`: `E G`; `C` to `D`: none; `D` to `G`: `D`.
3. Play the strings 3–2–1 path from the table (`7 8 7`, `9 8 7`, `9 8 8`, `7 7 5`) as block chords and say the common tones before each change.
4. Play the arpeggio table at 70 beats per minute, one bar per chord. Say each note name aloud as you play it.
5. Over a loop of `G – Em – C – D`, improvise at step 1 (chord tones only) for four loops. Landing on the third of each chord on beat 1 means `B`, `G`, `E`, `F♯`. Verify your landing notes.
6. Add passing tones at step 2. Mark in writing which of your notes were chord tones and which were passing.
7. Play `C – Am – F – G` using the smooth path from the previous lesson on strings 4–3–2 (`10 9 8`, `10 9 10`, `10 10 10`, `9 7 8`). Add a bass note under each chord: `C` on string 5, fret 3; `A` on string 5, open; `F` on string 6, fret 1; `G` on string 6, fret 3.
8. Play an `Em` triad (`9 8 7` on strings 3–2–1) over a `C` bass note (string 5, fret 3). Name the four pitch classes and describe the sound. Expected: `C E G B`, a major seventh color.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** Explain why chord tones fall on strong beats and passing tones between them, and why the third is the most informative note.
- **Written:** List the triad tones for each chord in a four-chord progression, mark common tones between neighbours, and identify the leading tone.
- **Fretboard:** Play a four-chord progression with triads on strings 3–2–1 using a smooth path, with the bass root added from the lower strings.
- **Aural:** Hear a line land on the third of each chord and tell whether it follows the changes or not.
- **Application:** Comp, outline, and improvise over one progression using only its triad tones and scale passing tones, in a steady rhythm.
