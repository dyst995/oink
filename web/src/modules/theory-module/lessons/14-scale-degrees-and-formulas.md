# Scale degrees and formulas

**Prerequisites:** Constructing the major scale  
**Next:** Keys, signatures, and the circle of fifths

## Concept

A **scale degree** is a note's position in the scale, counted from the root: `1 2 3 4 5 6 7`. A **formula** is a list of degrees, often with alterations, that describes a structure without naming any root.

The major scale is the reference. Its formula is:

`1 2 3 4 5 6 7`

Everything else is described by how it differs from that reference:

- `♭3` means degree 3 of the major scale lowered by a half step.
- `♯4` means degree 4 raised by a half step.
- `♭7` means degree 7 lowered by a half step.

A formula holds for any root. A note name holds for only one.

## Underlying logic

Formulas separate **structure** from **pitch**. `1 3 5` is "root, major third, fifth" in every key. Plug in a root and the notes follow. To translate, build the major scale from the root, then pick or alter degrees.

Alterations are always measured against the **major** scale on the same root, not against the previous note. In `A`: degree 3 is `C♯`, so `♭3` is `C`. Degree 7 is `G♯`, so `♭7` is `G`.

A degree number is also an interval name from the root: degree 3 is a third above, degree 5 is a fifth above. Quality (major, minor, perfect) comes from how many half steps the major scale gives it, then from the alteration. This is why a formula can be read in two ways, as scale positions or as intervals.

Degrees also have functional names that describe their role:

| Degree | Name | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `1` | Tonic | Home, rest |
| `2` | Supertonic | Above the tonic |
| `3` | Mediant | Halfway to the fifth; sets major or minor |
| `4` | Subdominant | Leans down toward 3 |
| `5` | Dominant | Strongest support for the tonic |
| `6` | Submediant | Relative minor lives here |
| `7` | Leading tone | Half step under tonic in major |

## Musical significance

Melodies and chords are understood as degree patterns, not as letters. "Sing 1–2–3–5" is the same musical idea in any key. A tune moved to a new key keeps its degrees but changes its letters.

Chords are formulas too. Major triad `1 3 5`, minor triad `1 ♭3 5`, dominant seventh `1 3 5 ♭7`. Learning formulas lets you build a chord or scale you have never seen on a root you just chose.

## Guitar application

Degrees have fixed fret relationships that travel with the root. Put the root on string 6 at fret `r` (with `r` at least 1):

- Degree 4: string 5, fret `r` (same fret, one string up).
- Degree 5: string 5, fret `r+2`.
- Octave (degree 8): string 4, fret `r+2`.
- Degree 3: string 5, fret `r−1`. `♭3`: string 5, fret `r−2`.

Check with `G` at string 6, fret 3: degree 3 is `B` at string 5, fret 2. Degree 5 is `D` at string 5, fret 5. Octave `G` is string 4, fret 5. Degree 4 is `C` at string 5, fret 3. All four match the notes in G major (`G A B C D E F♯`).

Because these relationships are relative, they work from any root fret.

Example translations in different keys:

| Formula | In `C` | In `G` | In `D` | In `F` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `1 3 5` | `C E G` | `G B D` | `D F♯ A` | `F A C` |
| `1 ♭3 5` | `C E♭ G` | `G B♭ D` | `D F A` | `F A♭ C` |
| `1 2 3 5` | `C D E G` | `G A B D` | `D E F♯ A` | `F G A C` |
| `1 3 ♯4 5` | `C E F♯ G` | `G B C♯ D` | `D F♯ G♯ A` | `F A B C` |

(The last row shows that `♯4` is a half step above degree 4: in `F` the fourth is `B♭`, so `♯4` is `B`.)

## Exercises

1. Write degrees `1–7` for `G`, `D`, `A`, and `F`. Expected for `G`: `G A B C D E F♯`. Expected for `F`: `F G A B♭ C D E`.
2. What is degree 5 in `E`, `A♭`, `B♭`, and `F♯`? Expected: `B`, `E♭`, `F`, `C♯`.
3. In `A`, name `1 ♭3 5 ♭7`. Expected: `A C E G`.
4. In `E`, name `1 2 ♭3 4 5`. Expected: `E F♯ G A B`.
5. Which degree of `D` major is `C♯`? Which degree of `B♭` major is `A`? Expected: 7, 7.
6. On guitar, root `C` on string 6, fret 8. Using the relative rules, find and name degree 3, 5, and the octave. Expected: string 5 fret 7 (`E`), string 5 fret 10 (`G`), string 4 fret 10 (`C`).
7. Play the root on string 6 and sing degree 5 aloud before you play it. Check by ear against your guess. Repeat from three different roots.
8. Describe what `♭7` of `E` is and where it is on string 6. Expected: `D`, string 6, fret 10.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** Explain why `♭3` is measured against the major scale, and why a formula is independent of the root.
- **Written:** Translate in both directions between note names and degree formulas in at least `C G D A E F B♭`.
- **Fretboard:** Given a root on string 6 or 5, locate degrees 3, 4, 5, and the octave without counting from open strings.
- **Aural:** Sing degree 5 or degree 3 from a played root, and identify `♭3` versus `3` by ear.
- **Application:** Read a formula such as `1 ♭3 5 ♭7` and name the notes in a chosen key without hesitation.
