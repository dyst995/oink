# Scales across the neck

**Prerequisites:** Major scale construction; scale degrees; locating pitch classes; intervals on the neck  
**Next:** Chord shapes as interval stacks

## Concept

Lay out a major scale by **construction**, not by treating a box diagram as the scale itself. From a tonic you know on the neck, place degrees `1 2 3 4 5 6 7` using whole/half steps and known pitch-class locations. Then connect into adjacent areas that share those same classes.

A “position” is a convenient hand span, not a prison. Link positions by noticing the same degree in two places (often an octave or a unison across strings).

Natural minor (preview): relative minor uses the same pitch classes as a major key starting from degree 6 — or the formula `1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7` from a minor tonic. Build it the same way: degrees first, boxes second.

## Examples

- `G` major: find `G` (1), walk WWHWWWH on one string, then refill the same degrees across strings nearby.
- Degree `3` in G is `B` — it appears in several frets; any of them is still degree 3 in that key.
- Connect two fingerings of G major by sliding or string-crossing on a shared `D` (degree 5).

## Exercises

1. Choose key `C`. Play one octave of the major scale ascending and descending while saying degree numbers.
2. In key `A`, play the scale across at least three strings without staying on one string for the whole octave.
3. Mark every `1` and `5` you can find for that key between frets 0–12. Play a short line that hits only those degrees, then add `2` and `3`.

**Done when:** you can play a major scale in a key while naming degrees, and you can move to a neighboring area using shared pitch classes instead of restarting from a diagram.
