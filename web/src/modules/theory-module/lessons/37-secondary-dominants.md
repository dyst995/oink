# Secondary dominants

**Prerequisites:** Functional progressions  
**Next:** Borrowed chords and modal interchange

## Concept

A **secondary dominant** is a dominant seventh chord that points at a chord of the key other than the tonic. It is written `V7/x`, "five of x". In `C` major, `A7` is `V7/ii` because `A` is a fifth above `D`, and `D` is the root of `ii`. The chord `A7` contains `C♯`, a note outside `C` major, and that note acts as a leading tone to `D`.

The chord being pointed at (the **target**) is **temporarily tonicized**: for a moment it feels like home, without the key actually changing.

## Underlying logic

### Construction

To build `V7/x`:

1. Choose a diatonic target chord (major or minor; the diminished chord is not a normal target).
2. Find the note a perfect fifth above the target's root (equivalently a perfect fourth below).
3. Build a dominant 7th chord on that note.

In `C` major:

| Secondary dominant | Chord | Target | Chromatic note (not in `C`) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `V7/ii` | `A7` | `Dm` | `C♯` |
| `V7/iii` | `B7` | `Em` | `D♯` |
| `V7/IV` | `C7` | `F` | `B♭` |
| `V7/V` | `D7` | `G` | `F♯` |
| `V7/vi` | `E7` | `Am` | `G♯` |

There is no `V7/vii°` in normal practice because a diminished target is unstable and not a convincing temporary home. `V7/I` is just `V7`.

### Why they work

The tritone inside a dominant 7 chord (third and seventh) seeks resolution, as in seventh-chord construction. For `A7` (`A C♯ E G`): `C♯` rises a half step to `D`, and `G` falls a half step to `F`. Both land on notes of `Dm` (`D F A`). The chromatic note is the **leading tone** of the target. Because the target is diatonic and the surrounding chords still belong to `C`, the ear hears a brief detour and not a new key.

`C7` as `V7/IV` is the subtle case. It contains `E`, which rises to `F`, and `B♭`, which falls to `A` (the third of `F`). The `B♭` is also the note that distinguishes `C7` from `Cmaj7` and from the plain `C` triad.

### Tonicization is not modulation

A tonicization is a single chord or short phrase aimed at a target. **Modulation** is when the new key is confirmed by more material, such as a full cadence in the new key and a lasting return to its scale. The line between them is judged by ear and context. A practical test: if the original key still feels like home after the target chord passes, it was a tonicization.

### Not every dominant 7 is a secondary dominant

A `D7` in `C` major could be `V7/V` (resolving to `G`), but it could also be a color chord or a blues-style chord with no target. The label requires a resolution to the target, or a clear expectation of one that is then deceived. Always check what the chord does next.

## Musical significance

Secondary dominants add chromatic color and strong motion without leaving the key. A standard `I vi ii V` turnaround often becomes `I VI7 ii V`: the `vi` is replaced by `VI7` (which is `V7/ii`), creating a chain `VI7 to ii to V to I` in which each chord points at the next. In `C` that is `C A7 Dm G7 C`.

Chains of secondary dominants are common in jazz standards and older popular songs. In rock and folk, a single `V7/V` (`D7` in `C`) before `G` is a typical use.

## Guitar application

Shapes for `C` major (strings 6 to 1):

| Chord | Frets | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `A7` | `x02020` | A E G C♯ E |
| `B7` | `x21202` | B D♯ A B F♯ |
| `C7` | `x32310` | C E B♭ C E |
| `D7` | `xx0212` | D A C F♯ |
| `E7` | `020100` | E B D G♯ B E |

Try the progression `C A7 Dm G7 C`: `x32010`, then `x02020`, then `xx0231`, then `320001`, then `x32010`.

Look for the half-step motion from `A7` (`x02020`) to `Dm` (`xx0231`):

- The chromatic note `C♯` (string 2 fret 2) moves up to `D` on the same string (fret 3): the leading tone reaches the root of the target.
- The open high `E` (string 1) moves up to `F` (string 1 fret 1): the fifth of `A7` becomes the third of `Dm`.

For a shell version, use the sevenths-on-guitar shell offsets with the root on string 5: `A7` is `x0x02x` (`A G C♯`) and `Dm7` is `x5x56x` (`D C F`). The `C♯` still leads to `D`, but now across strings and registers, which is a reminder that leading-tone motion is about pitch classes, not about staying on one string.

## Exercises

1. Write all five secondary dominants of `G` major with their targets. Answer: `V7/ii = E7` (to `Am`), `V7/iii = F♯7` (to `Bm`), `V7/IV = G7` (to `C`), `V7/V = A7` (to `D`), `V7/vi = B7` (to `Em`).
2. Write `I VI7 ii V I` in `C`, `G`, and `F`. Answers: `C A7 Dm G7 C`; `G E7 Am D7 G`; `F D7 Gm C7 F`.
3. Play `C D7 G C`. Say which chord is the target of `D7` and name the non-diatonic note in `D7` (`F♯`).
4. Play `C E7 Am`, then `C E7 F`. Explain, in terms of chord tones, why the first resolves strongly (`G♯` to `A`) and the second does not.
5. Play `C A7 Dm`, then `C Am Dm`. Describe by ear what the `C♯` adds.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** explain a secondary dominant as a dominant 7th aimed at a diatonic chord that is not `I`, and explain tonicization versus modulation.
- **Written:** for any major key, name the five secondary dominants and their targets.
- **Fretboard:** play a `V7/ii` to `ii` to `V7` to `I` progression in two keys, naming the chromatic note in each secondary dominant.
- **Aural:** hear a secondary dominant as a brief extra leading tone in a familiar progression, such as `C A7 Dm G7 C`.
- **Application:** add one secondary dominant to a diatonic four-chord progression and explain what it targets.
