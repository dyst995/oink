# Modes in context

**Prerequisites:** Scale degrees and formulas; functional progressions; scales across the neck  
**Next:** Fretboard harmony and voice leading

## Concept

A **mode** of major is the same W/H sequence starting from a different degree — or a seven-note formula with a characteristic color against a tonic that is **not** behaving like major-key I–V–I.

Parent major modes (from major degrees):

| Mode | From major degree | Characteristic idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ionian | 1 | Major scale itself |
| Dorian | 2 | Minor with raised 6 (`1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7`) |
| Phrygian | 3 | Minor with ♭2 |
| Lydian | 4 | Major with ♯4 |
| Mixolydian | 5 | Major with ♭7 |
| Aeolian | 6 | Natural minor |
| Locrian | 7 | Diminished tonic flavor (`♭2` and `♭5`) |

**Context matters.** Mixolydian fits a dominant (or dominant vamp) centered on that tonic. Dorian fits a minor vamp that wants a raised 6. If the harmony is functional major with clear V7→I, plain Ionian/major language is usually the default — don’t force a mode name onto ordinary major-key music.

Build a mode from a formula from the sounding tonic, or by rotating the parent major scale and treating a non-1 degree as home. Then play it against a vamp that actually supports that home.

## Examples

- `G` Mixolydian: `G A B C D E F` — G major with `F` natural; fits a G7 groove.
- `D` Dorian: `D E F G A B C` — from C major starting on D; fits a Dm vamp that includes `B` natural.
- A song in C major with G7–C is not “Mixolydian on G” for the whole form; G7 is V in C.

## Exercises

1. Write Mixolydian and Dorian formulas. Spell each from tonic `A`.
2. Improvise on an A7 vamp with A Mixolydian (use `G` natural, not `G♯`).
3. For a progression `C – G – Am – F`, argue whether labeling a mode on every chord helps or whether key-of-C degrees are enough.

**Done when:** you can build a mode from a formula or parent scale and pick Mixolydian vs Ionian (or Dorian vs Aeolian) for a vamp that actually centers that sound.
