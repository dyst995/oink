# Chord substitutions

**Prerequisites:** Functional progressions; secondary dominants  
**Next:** Modes in context

## Concept

A **chord substitution** replaces one chord with another that does a similar job. The substitute keeps enough of the original's notes, function, or voice leading that the progression still makes sense. Substitution is a tool for variety and color. It is not a rule; always judge the result by ear and against the melody.

Common practical substitutions:

- **Relative substitution:** `I` to `vi` or `iii`, `IV` to `ii`.
- **Adding or changing quality:** triad to seventh, `V` to `V7`, `V7` to a suspended or ninth version.
- **Dominant substitution:** `V7` to `viiø7` (a rootless dominant), or a **tritone substitution**.
- **Same-function swap:** another predominant chord instead of `IV`.

## Underlying logic

### Shared notes

The easiest test is to count shared pitch classes. In `C` major:

| Original | Substitute | Shared notes | Typical effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `C` (`C E G`) | `Am` (`A C E`) | `C E` | Softer, more melancholy tonic |
| `C` (`C E G`) | `Em` (`E G B`) | `E G` | Lighter tonic, weaker arrival |
| `F` (`F A C`) | `Dm` (`D F A`) | `F A` | Predominant stays; the bass moves from `F` to `D` |
| `Fmaj7` (`F A C E`) | `Dm7` (`D F A C`) | `F A C` | Three shared notes; similar sound, different bass |
| `G7` (`G B D F`) | `Bm7♭5` (`B D F A`) | `B D F` | Dominant function stays; the root `G` is missing |

The last pair is the idea of the **rootless dominant**: `Bm7♭5` is `G7` without its root, plus `A` (the ninth of `G`). Over a bass note `G`, a `Bm7♭5` voicing sounds as `G9`; without a `G` in the bass it is a separate chord.

Function usually survives when the two chords share most of their notes, especially the notes that carry the pull (the leading tone, or the third and seventh of a dominant), and the new chord does not suggest a different tonal center.

### Tritone substitution (introductory)

A dominant seventh has a tritone between its third and seventh. In `G7` that tritone is `B` and `F`. Another dominant seventh chord contains the same two notes: `D♭7` (`D♭ F A♭ C♭`, where `C♭` sounds the same as `B`). Same tritone, different roots: `G` and `D♭` are a tritone apart (six semitones).

So in `G7 to Cmaj7` (`V7 to I`), you can play `D♭7 to Cmaj7`. The root motion becomes a half step down (`D♭` to `C`) instead of a fifth down (`G` to `C`), and the tritone's notes resolve as before: `B` (as `C♭`) to `C`, and `F` to `E`.

Cautions:

- The tritone sub swaps the root. Melody notes that fit `G7` may clash. For example, the melody note `D` (the fifth of `G7`) sits a half step above `D♭`, a strong `♭9` color, and `G` (the root of `G7`) is a `♯11` over `D♭7`. Both can sound good or bad; test with the melody.
- It is a dominant substitute. It should replace a dominant 7th that resolves down a fifth (or stands for one). It does not replace `I7` or `IV7` in a blues.
- It works best when the next chord is a half step below the substitute's root, which makes the bass move chromatically.

### When not to substitute

- When the melody holds the note you would remove or alter.
- When the original chord is the structural arrival of a cadence.
- When it makes the form unclear to other players.

## Musical significance

Substitution lets you refresh familiar harmony without leaving the key's structure. Beginner-friendly uses are `I` to `vi` in a loop, triads to sevenths, and `V7` colored with extensions. Tritone substitution and rootless voicings belong to jazz and jazz-influenced writing, where the bass part is often independent of the chord voicing.

## Guitar application

Compare these `C` shapes (strings 6 to 1):

- `C` `x32010` and `Am7` `x02010` (one note differs: the bass `C` versus `A`).
- `Fmaj7` `xx3210` and `Dm7` `xx0211`.
- `G7` `320001` and `Bm7♭5` `x2323x`.

Tritone sub with shells:

- `G7` shell, root on string 6: `3x34xx` (`G F B`).
- `D♭7` shell, root on string 5: `x4x46x` (`D♭ B F`). The `B` and `F` are the same pitch classes as in `G7`.
- `Cmaj7` shell, root on string 5: `x3x45x` (`C B E`).

From `D♭7` shell `x4x46x` to `Cmaj7` shell `x3x45x`, the root `D♭` goes to `C` (string 5 fret 4 to 3), the `B` stays on string 3 fret 4, and `F` goes to `E` (string 2 fret 6 to 5). Only the root and the `F` move, each by a half step. That is the smooth voice leading a tritone sub is known for.

## Exercises

1. List which notes `Am` and `C` share, and which notes `Em` and `C` share. Play `C Am F G` and `C Em F G` and compare the feeling of the first chord.
2. In `C` major, `Cmaj7` is `C E G B` and `Am7` is `A C E G`. Name the three shared notes (`C E G`) and say what the substitute adds and removes (adds `A`, removes `B`).
3. Write the notes of `D7` and of `A♭7`, and identify the tritone they share. Answer: `D7 = D F♯ A C`; `A♭7 = A♭ C E♭ G♭`; the shared tritone is `F♯` (which sounds the same as `G♭`) and `C`.
4. Write the tritone substitute for `D7` in `D7 to Gmaj7`, and for `E7` in `E7 to Am7`. Answers: `A♭7` and `B♭7`.
5. Play `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7` with shells (`x5x56x`, `3x34xx`, `x3x45x`), then `Dm7 D♭7 Cmaj7` (`x5x56x`, `x4x46x`, `x3x45x`). In the second version, from `Dm7` to `D♭7` the `F` on string 2 fret 6 stays while the other two notes drop a fret, and from `D♭7` to `Cmaj7` the `B` on string 3 stays while the other two drop a fret. Say what the bass does in each version (fifths down versus chromatic descent).

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** explain a substitution as keeping function through shared notes or shared voice leading.
- **Conceptual:** explain a tritone substitution as swapping dominant chords that share their third and seventh.
- **Written:** list shared notes for three relative substitution pairs and two dominant substitution pairs.
- **Fretboard:** play a `ii V I` with both `G7` and `D♭7` shells and identify which voices move by half step.
- **Aural:** hear a tritone substitute and describe the chromatic bass motion.
- **Application:** choose one substitution in a progression, justify it with shared notes, and check it against a melody.
