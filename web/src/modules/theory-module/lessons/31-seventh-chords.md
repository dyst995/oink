# Seventh chords

**Prerequisites:** Triad construction  
**Next:** Sevenths on the guitar

## Concept

A **seventh chord** is a triad plus a fourth note a third above the fifth. It has four chord tones: root, third, fifth, seventh. Five qualities cover almost everything you will meet in tonal guitar music:

| Name | Symbols | Formula | Semitones from root | Stacked thirds (bottom to top) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major 7 | `Cmaj7`, `CΔ7` | `1 3 5 7` | 0 4 7 11 | major, minor, major |
| Dominant 7 | `C7` | `1 3 5 ♭7` | 0 4 7 10 | major, minor, minor |
| Minor 7 | `Cm7` | `1 ♭3 5 ♭7` | 0 3 7 10 | minor, major, minor |
| Half-diminished | `Cm7♭5`, `Cø7` | `1 ♭3 ♭5 ♭7` | 0 3 6 10 | minor, minor, major |
| Diminished 7 | `Cdim7`, `C°7` | `1 ♭3 ♭5 ♭♭7` | 0 3 6 9 | minor, minor, minor |

Spell a seventh chord in two steps: stack letter names in thirds (`C E G B`), then apply accidentals to match the formula. In `Cdim7` the seventh is `B♭♭`, which sounds the same as `A`, but the letter name `B` keeps the stack in thirds.

**Quality name and function are different things.** "Dominant 7" names a sound: a major triad with a minor seventh. A chord with that sound does not always have dominant function in the key. That distinction matters once diatonic seventh harmony and functional progressions arrive.

## Underlying logic

Every seventh chord is a triad (`1 3 5` with its quality) plus one more interval above the root:

- Major triad + major 7th (`11` semitones) gives maj7.
- Major triad + minor 7th (`10`) gives dominant 7.
- Minor triad + minor 7th (`10`) gives m7.
- Diminished triad + minor 7th (`10`) gives m7♭5.
- Diminished triad + diminished 7th (`9`) gives dim7.

### Where the tension of a dominant 7 comes from

In `G7` (`G B D F`) the third (`B`) and the seventh (`F`) are six semitones apart: a **tritone**. The tritone is unstable. It resolves most smoothly when each note moves a half step in opposite directions: `B` up to `C`, `F` down to `E`. Those two destinations are the root and third of `C`. That is why `G7` pulls toward `C` major or `C` minor harmony, and why `V7` to `I` is the model resolution.

The major 7th has a different character. In `Cmaj7` (`C E G B`) `B` sits a half step below the root's octave. This gives a bright, slightly rubbing sound rather than a pull to somewhere else.

### The symmetry of dim7

All three stacked thirds in `dim7` are minor thirds (three semitones), so the chord divides the octave into four equal parts. This gives only three distinct `dim7` chords, each nameable from any of its four notes:

- `C E♭ G♭ A` (`Cdim7`, `E♭dim7`, `G♭dim7`, `Adim7`)
- `C♯ E G B♭` (`C♯dim7`, `Edim7`, `Gdim7`, `B♭dim7`)
- `D F A♭ B` (`Ddim7`, `Fdim7`, `A♭dim7`, `Bdim7`)

So "the root" of a `dim7` is decided by context, not by the notes alone. This is one place where "lowest note equals root" is unreliable.

## Musical significance

- **maj7** is stable. It is common as `I` or `IV` in major keys and gives a softer tonic than a plain triad.
- **m7** is common as `ii`, `iii`, and `vi` in major, and as `i` or `iv` in minor.
- **7** (dominant quality) usually carries the strongest forward pull when it is `V7`. It also appears as `I7` and `IV7` in blues, where it is a color of the style and not a chord waiting to resolve.
- **m7♭5** is the usual `ii` chord in minor keys and the `vii` chord of the major scale (in major it is often a stand-in for `V7`).
- **dim7** is usually a chromatic connector. It tends to resolve by half step toward a nearby chord.

## Guitar application

Chord diagrams below list strings 6 to 1 (low E to high E). `x` means the string is not played and `0` means open.

| Chord | Frets (6 to 1) | Notes, low to high |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Cmaj7` | `x32000` | C E G B E |
| `G7` | `320001` | G B D G B F |
| `Am7` | `x02010` | A E G C E |
| `Dm7` | `xx0211` | D A C F |
| `Em7` | `022030` | E B E G D E |
| `Bm7♭5` | `x2323x` | B F A D |
| `Bdim7` | `x2313x` | B F A♭ D |

Notice the `Bdim7` shape. Move it up three frets (`x5646x`) and you get `Ddim7`, which has the same four pitch classes as `Bdim7`. Moving the same shape any multiple of three frets gives the same chord with a different note in the bass.

For `Cmaj7` (`x32000`), listen for the half step between the open `B` string and the `C` on the fifth string, third fret. That is the major 7th rub.

## Exercises

1. Spell `maj7`, `7`, and `m7` chords on `A` and on `E♭`. Answer key: `Amaj7 A C♯ E G♯`, `A7 A C♯ E G`, `Am7 A C E G`; `E♭maj7 E♭ G B♭ D`, `E♭7 E♭ G B♭ D♭`, `E♭m7 E♭ G♭ B♭ D♭`.
2. In `G7` name the interval between the third and the seventh, and say in half steps how large it is (answer: `B` to `F`, a tritone, six semitones). Do the same for `D7` and `E7`.
3. Play `G7` (`320001`) and slowly add the resolving chord `Cmaj7` (`x32000`). Identify by ear and on the neck where `B` moves to `C` and `F` moves to `E`.
4. Compare three chords from the same root: play `Gmaj7` (`320002`), `G7` (`320001`), and `Gm7` (`353333`). Name the one note that differs between each pair.
5. Play `Bdim7` (`x2313x`), slide it up three frets, and say aloud the four pitch classes before and after.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** explain the difference between chord quality and chord function in your own words, using `G7` in `C` and an `A7` in a 12-bar blues as examples.
- **Written:** spell all five qualities from any root in under ten seconds each, using letter-name stacking plus formula.
- **Fretboard:** play at least one voicing each of `maj7`, `7`, `m7`, and `m7♭5` and name the chord tones on each string.
- **Aural:** distinguish `maj7`, `7`, and `m7` from the same root, played on guitar, at least eight times out of ten.
- **Application:** explain why the tritone in `G7` resolves toward `C`, pointing to the two voices that move a half step.
