# Borrowed chords

**Prerequisites:** Diatonic harmony; functional progressions  
**Next:** Chord substitutions

## Concept

A **borrowed chord** (also called a **modal interchange** chord) is a chord taken from a different mode or scale that shares the same tonic as the home key. The most common source is the **parallel minor**: the minor key with the same tonic. In `C` major, the parallel minor is `C` minor (natural minor: `C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭`). Chords built from that scale but used inside a `C` major progression are borrowed.

Borrowed chords differ from secondary dominants. A secondary dominant adds a chromatic leading tone to aim at a diatonic target. A borrowed chord changes the scale color around the tonic.

## Underlying logic

### Where the chords come from

Natural minor on `C` gives these chords:

| Degree | Triad | Seventh | Numeral (relative to the major scale) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `Cm` | `Cm7` | `i` |
| 2 | `D°` | `Dm7♭5` | `ii°` |
| 3 | `E♭` | `E♭maj7` | `♭III` |
| 4 | `Fm` | `Fm7` | `iv` |
| 5 | `Gm` | `Gm7` | `v` |
| 6 | `A♭` | `A♭maj7` | `♭VI` |
| 7 | `B♭` | `B♭7` | `♭VII` |

The numerals `♭III`, `♭VI`, `♭VII` mean the root is a half step lower than the major-scale degree. The case still shows the chord quality (`♭VII` is major).

The most-used borrowed chords in major:

- **iv** (`Fm` in `C`): the minor chord on the fourth degree. It contains `A♭`, a note outside `C` major.
- **♭VII** (`B♭` in `C`): a major chord a whole step below the tonic.
- **♭VI** (`A♭` in `C`): a major chord whose root is a minor sixth above the tonic.
- **♭III** (`E♭` in `C`): a major chord a minor third above the tonic.

### Two explanations for ♭VII

`♭VII` can be explained as borrowed from parallel natural minor, or as a chord of **Mixolydian** (the major scale with a lowered 7th: `C D E F G A B♭`), which also contains `B♭`. Both explanations correctly describe where the note lives. The first treats `♭VII` as a coloring inside a major key; the second treats the passage as a Mixolydian sound. Which you prefer depends on how much of the music has the lowered seventh.

### Behavior

- **iv** keeps a predominant function. Its `A♭` falls a half step to `G` (the fifth of `C`, the root of `G`). `F` to `Fm` to `C` sounds like the major `IV` darkened.
- **♭VII** often moves to `I`, or to `IV` first: `♭VII IV I` or `I ♭VII IV I`. Its `B♭` can fall a half step to `A` (the third of `F`, when `♭VII` goes to `IV`) or rise a whole step to `C` (when it goes to `I`).
- **♭VI** often precedes `♭VII` and then `I` (`A♭ B♭ C`). This arrives on `I` without a leading tone (the root moves `B♭` to `C` by whole step, not by half step), and it is common in rock and film-style writing.
- Borrowed chords sound colorful because their lowered notes (`E♭`, `A♭`, `B♭`) are not in the major scale.

### From major into minor

Minor keys borrow in the other direction too:

- The **Picardy third**: ending a minor piece on a major `I`.
- A major `IV` in a minor key (`i IV` in `A`: `Am D`), a Dorian color.
- A major `V` in a minor key (harmonic minor), which is so standard that it is usually not described as borrowed.

## Musical significance

Borrowed chords darken, brighten, or expand the harmonic palette without a key change. In major keys, `iv` and `♭VI` give a wistful or dramatic mood. `♭VII` gives an open, rock-like flavor. You can hear the change by playing the same melody against `IV` and then `iv`.

Do not treat these chords as exceptions to memorise separately. Each is a diatonic chord of a nearby scale. The key does not change; the mood does.

## Guitar application

Chords for `C` (strings 6 to 1):

| Chord | Frets | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Fm` | `133111` | F C F A♭ C F |
| `A♭` | `466544` | A♭ E♭ A♭ C E♭ A♭ |
| `B♭` | `x13331` | B♭ F B♭ D F |
| `E♭` | `x68886` | E♭ B♭ E♭ G B♭ |

Progressions to play and compare:

1. `C F C`, then `C Fm C`. Listen to `A♭` appear.
2. `C B♭ F C` (`I ♭VII IV I`).
3. `C A♭ B♭ C` (`I ♭VI ♭VII I`).

In `A` minor, the major `IV`: `Am D Am` with `D` as `xx0232`.

Find the borrowed note on one string. In `C`, the `A` on string 1 is fret 5 and the `A♭` is fret 4. Play the `C` major scale, then play it with `A♭` in place of `A`: that lowered sixth degree is the flavor of `iv` and `♭VI`.

## Exercises

1. Write the parallel-minor scale for `C`, `G`, and `D`. Answers: `C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭`; `G A B♭ C D E♭ F`; `D E F G A B♭ C`.
2. List the borrowed `iv`, `♭III`, `♭VI`, and `♭VII` in `G` major. Answer: `Cm`, `B♭`, `E♭`, `F`.
3. Play `C F C` and `C Fm C` and say which scale degree changes (the sixth degree, `A` versus `A♭`).
4. Play `I ♭VII IV I` in `D`: `D C G D`. Explain what `C` natural is doing against the `D` major scale (the lowered seventh).
5. Write four bars using `I`, `IV`, `iv`, and `I` in `E`. Identify the borrowed note. Answer: `E A Am E`; the borrowed chord `Am` contains `C` natural, which is not in `E` major (which has `C♯`).

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** explain modal interchange as borrowing chords from a scale that shares the same tonic.
- **Conceptual:** explain why `♭VII` can be described as borrowed or as Mixolydian, and say how borrowing differs from a secondary dominant.
- **Written:** list the borrowed chords from parallel minor for any major key.
- **Fretboard:** play `iv`, `♭VI`, and `♭VII` in a key of your choice with barre shapes.
- **Aural:** distinguish `IV` from `iv` and `V` from `♭VII` by ear.
- **Application:** write a progression using one borrowed chord and explain the effect and the borrowed note.
