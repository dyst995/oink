# Pentatonic and blues as subsets

**Prerequisites:** Scale degrees and formulas; scales across the neck  
**Next:** Modes in context

## Concept

**Pentatonic** scales are five-note subsets of seven-note parents — not a parallel universe.

- **Major pentatonic:** `1 2 3 5 6` (major scale without 4 and 7).
- **Minor pentatonic:** `1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7` (natural minor without 2 and ♭6).

Relative major/minor pentatonics share pitch classes: `A` minor pentatonic = `C` major pentatonic starting from `A`.

The **blues scale** commonly adds a chromatic passing tone to minor pentatonic: `1 ♭3 4 ♭5 5 ♭7`. The `♭5` is color against a tonic or dominant groove, not a new key system by itself.

Use them knowingly: minor pentatonic over a minor tonic or blues/rock dominant loops; major pentatonic for major-key melodic outlines. Against functional changes, still prefer chord tones at the landings.

## Examples

- `A` minor pentatonic: `A C D E G`.
- `C` major pentatonic: `C D E G A` — same set as above.
- `A` blues: `A C D E♭ E G`.

## Exercises

1. Derive major pentatonic from `G` major by removing degrees 4 and 7. Play it while saying the remaining degrees.
2. Play `E` minor pentatonic, then add `♭5` for blues. Use both over a static E7 vamp if you can.
3. Over `| G | C | D | G |`, try major pentatonic of G, then switch to outlining each chord’s triad on bar changes — notice the difference.

**Done when:** you can write pentatonic formulas from major/minor parents and choose major vs minor pentatonic for a clear harmonic situation.
