# Scale degrees and formulas

**Prerequisites:** Keys and tonality  
**Next:** Interval names and quality

## Concept

Inside a key, each pitch class of the major scale has a **scale degree** number relative to the tonic:

| Degree | Role (name) |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Tonic |
| 2 | Supertonic |
| 3 | Mediant |
| 4 | Subdominant |
| 5 | Dominant |
| 6 | Submediant |
| 7 | Leading tone |

You need the numbers more than the fancy names at first. Degree `1` is always the tonic of the key you are in. Degree `5` is always a fifth up the major scale from that tonic — the pitch class changes when the key changes, the **role** does not.

A **formula** writes a collection as degrees instead of fixed note names. The major scale itself is:

`1 2 3 4 5 6 7`

That formula is true in every major key. In `C` it spells `C D E F G A B`. In `A` it spells `A B C♯ D E F♯ G♯`. Same formula, different pitch classes.

Later, minor scales, pentatonics, and chords are other formulas (`1 ♭3 5`, `1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7`, …) applied to a tonic. Learning to think in degrees is how one idea moves across the neck and across keys without relearning twelve separate alphabets.

Accidentals in a formula (`♭3`, `♯4`) mean: take that major-scale degree and lower or raise it one half step. They are alterations of the major reference, not random decorations.

## Examples

- Formula `1 2 3 4 5 6 7` in `G`: `G A B C D E F♯`.
- Formula `1 3 5` in `G`: `G B D` (a G major triad — preview only).
- Degree `7` in `C` is `B`; in `F` it is `E`. Different notes, same job: lead toward `1`.
- Mapping: if someone says “play degree 4 in A major,” they mean `D`, not “the fourth fret.”

## Exercises

1. In `D` major, write the pitch class for each degree `1` through `7`.
2. Translate this melody of degrees into pitch classes in `C` and again in `A`: `1 2 3 5 1`.
3. Given `F A C` in the key of `F` major, name the degrees. Then find the same degrees in `B♭` major and write the pitch classes.

**Done when:** you can move freely between “note names in a key” and “degree formula,” and you treat `1 2 3 4 5 6 7` as the reusable description of any major key.
