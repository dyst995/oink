# Improvising through changes

**Prerequisites:** Arpeggios; pentatonic; functional progressions  
**Next:** Melody, motif, and phrasing

## Concept

Improvising through changes means making up melody that fits a chord progression as it moves. The method in this lesson has three layers:

1. **Chord tones first.** Know the tones of each chord and use them on strong beats.
2. **Approach notes.** Reach a chord tone by stepping to it from above, below, or both, so the arrival is clear and the line is smooth.
3. **Scale as a resource.** A scale is a pool of fitting notes that fills the space between targets. It is not a rule that tells you what to play.

## Underlying logic

### Strong beats and weak beats

In `4/4`, beats 1 and 3 are usually stronger than beats 2 and 4, and a beat is stronger than its off-beat subdivision (see subdivisions, syncopation, and feel). Notes you land on or stay on carry more weight. Put **chord tones on strong beats** and let non-chord tones sit on weak beats or move quickly. A non-chord tone on a strong beat creates tension, which is a legitimate effect but needs to resolve.

### Approach notes

An approach note is a non-chord note that leads by step into a chord tone (the **target**).

| Type | Description | Example target `E` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chromatic below | half step from below | `D♯` to `E` |
| Chromatic above | half step from above | `F` to `E` |
| Diatonic | scale step, above or below | `D` to `E` or `F` to `E` in `C` major |
| Enclosure | one approach from above, one from below, then the target | `F` then `D♯` then `E` |

On the `B` string (string 2): `F` is fret 6, `D♯` is fret 4, and `E` is fret 5. The enclosure is `6 4 5`. In this example both neighbors are a half step from the target, so it arrives clearly. Enclosures can also use a scale step on one side.

### The scale as a resource, not a rule

There is no single scale that "goes with" a chord in every context. Use these questions instead:

- **Which key am I in?** In a `ii V I` in `C`, the `C` major scale contains every chord tone of `Dm7`, `G7`, and `Cmaj7`.
- **Which notes does the chord alter?** On `A7` in a blues in `A`, the third is `C♯`; many blues players also use the minor third `C` as a deliberate color against it (the "blue" note). That clash is part of the idiom, not a mistake.
- **Which scale tones are unstable?** Over `Cmaj7`, the fourth (`F`) sits a half step above the third (`E`). It works as a passing note and is riskier as a held note.

Treat chord-scale pairings you find elsewhere as suggestions about available notes and color, not laws.

## Musical significance

Players who outline chord tones and approach them well sound as if they are "playing the changes". Players who only run a single scale can still sound musical when the harmony is simple, but the changes become audible only when strong beats carry chord tones.

## Guitar application

### A stepwise method

1. **Loop two chords** (`Dm7` and `G7` in `C`). Play only the root of each on the first beat of each bar. Then play only the third (`F` then `B`).
2. **Add arpeggios** (previous lesson): state each chord in the first two beats, then fill the rest with scale notes.
3. **Add approach notes.** Pick a target for each change and approach it by half step from below, then from above, then enclose it.
4. **Add rhythm.** Repeat one short rhythm across all the chords before changing it.
5. **Then play freely**, listening for the target tone at each change.

### Specific example

Over `Dm7` to `G7` in `C`, target the `B` (third of `G7`).

- On string 1, `B` is fret 7, `B♭` is fret 6, and `C` is fret 8.
- From below: `B♭` (6) to `B` (7).
- From above: `C` (8) to `B` (7). The `C` is the seventh of `Dm7`, so this is also the guide-tone step from the guide-tones lesson.
- Enclosure: `C` (8), `B♭` (6), `B` (7).

Over `G7` to `Cmaj7`, target the `E` on the `B` string: `F` (6), `D♯` (4), `E` (5).

### Over a blues in `A`

Targets at each change: `A7` third `C♯` (string 2 fret 2), `D7` third `F♯` (string 1 fret 2), `E7` third `G♯` (string 1 fret 4). Between those, use `A` minor pentatonic notes as filler (`A C D E G`) and decide when you want the `C` and when you want the `C♯`.

## Exercises

1. Loop `Dm7` / `G7` / `Cmaj7`. For each chord, play one chord tone on the downbeat of each bar and rest: first roots, then thirds, then sevenths.
2. For the same loop, play four-note arpeggios in eighth notes, then use one approach note into each downbeat.
3. Choose one target (`E` on `Cmaj7`) and play the three approaches on string 2: from below (`D♯` to `E`, frets 4 to 5), from above (`F` to `E`, frets 6 to 5), and the enclosure (`F`, `D♯`, `E`, frets 6, 4, 5).
4. Record a 12-bar blues in `A`. On bar 5 (`D7`), land on `F♯` on beat 1. On bar 9 (`E7`), land on `G♯` on beat 1. Play whatever you like between.
5. Improvise over `Am7 D7 Gmaj7`. Name your target at each change before you play: `Am7` third `C`, `D7` third `F♯`, `Gmaj7` third `B`.
6. Play the same loop using only chord tones for one chorus, then only scale notes with no chord tone on any downbeat. Describe how the changes sound in each version.

## Mastery criteria

- **Conceptual:** explain chord tones first, approach notes, and scale as resource in your own words, and say why one scale per chord is a guideline only.
- **Written:** write a target note (third or seventh) for each chord in a progression and an approach into it.
- **Fretboard:** play three kinds of approach into a chosen chord tone at two locations on the neck.
- **Aural:** hear when a note on a strong beat clashes and when it resolves.
- **Application:** play an eight-bar improvisation over a loop in which each chord change lands on a chord tone, recorded and checked afterwards.
